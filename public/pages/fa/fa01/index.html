Chapter 1: Normed Spaces

  • What is a vector space?
  • What is a norm and what is a normed space?
  • What are some examples of normed spaces? $(\mathbb{R}^{n},\lVert \cdot \rVert_{e})$, $(\mathbb{R}^{n},\lVert \cdot \rVert_{1})$, $(\mathbb{R}^{n},\lVert \cdot \rVert_{\infty})$, $(\ell^{1}(\mathbb{N}),\lVert \cdot \rVert_{1})$, $(\mathscr{C}(I),\lVert \cdot \rVert_{1})$, $(\mathscr{C}(I),\lVert \cdot \rVert_{\infty})$

A normed space is a space on which you can define a norm. Now, we should try to understand what is a space and what is a norm. Before diving in, note that this is not the most general possible framework. More general are topological spaces, inside them we find metric spaces. But since they present some strange pathological things we start directly from the even smaller subset of normed spaces.

We assume the space (set) of elements we are dealing with is a vector space. In practice we will always just use $\mathbb{R}$ vector spaces, but the theory can be extended for $\mathbb{C}$.

What is a vector space? A collection of elements with two operations.

[D.][Vector Space]
A $\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}$ (or $\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}$) vector space, where $\mathbb{K}$ is a field and $X$ is a non-empty set on which we can define two operations:
  • Sum (addition): given $x,y\in X$ we can define a third element $x+y\in X$ which is the sum of $x$ and $y$. Note: the third element “$x+y$” is treated as a single entity/object, if you want you can call it $z=x+y$.
  • Scalar multiplication: given $X\in X, \alpha\in \mathbb{K}$ we can define $\alpha\cdot x\in X$ as the product of $\alpha\in \mathbb{K}$ with $x\in X$. Again, the idea is that $\alpha \cdot x$ is a new single element and it still belongs to $X$. This view will be very useful in the exercises we will see below.

These operations satisfy the following properties (note that these are part of the definition):
[0] Closed $+$ and $\bullet$: if you take $x\in X,y\in X$ the operation is still inside the set ie. $x+y\in X$ and if $x\in X,\alpha\in \mathbb{K}\Rightarrow \alpha\cdot x\in X$ (ie. all elements are still inside the set when scaled).

[1] Commutativity of $+$: $x+y=y+x\ \forall \ x,y\in X$. So if you first sum one or the other at the end you have the same element. Note that many, operations in life are not commutative. It’s not the same if you put first your underwear and then your pants than the other way around. In the first case you will be considered as a normal person. In the second a bizarre or crazy one.

[2] Associativity of $+$: $(x+y)+z=x+(y+z)\ \forall \ z,y\in X$. Ie. the order of the sum doesn’t even count if you make the sum of 3 or more elements.

[3] Neutral element of $\bullet$: there is a neutral element with respect to the sum that we call $0_{X}\in X$ “the zero of $X$” or “the origin of the space $X$” such that $0_{X}+x=x+0_{X}=x,\ \forall \ x\in X$. So if you sum it you get the element itself.

[4] Unique opposite: there is a unique element $y=-x\in X$ (this is just notation) such that $x+y=0_{X}=y+x\ \forall \ x\in X$. Or in other words $x+z_{x}=0_{X}=z_{x}+x$ and we write $z_{x}=-x$. There is no subtraction, just the sum of the opposite. [$\color{lime}\textbf{Q}$. In which sense? What is the difference in practice?]

[5] Commutativity of the product $\bullet$:

$$\alpha \stackrel{(1)}{\cdot } (\beta \stackrel{(2)}{\cdot } x)=(\alpha \stackrel{(3)}{\cdot } \beta)\stackrel{(4)}{\cdot }x$$

where (1) is a product in $X$, (2) is a product in $X$, (3) is a product in $\mathbb{K}$ and (4) is a product in $X$ [$\color{lime}\textbf{Q}$. What does it mean?] On the left we scale $x$ by $\beta$ and then scale $\beta x$ with $\alpha$. On the right side we multiply the scalars and then scale $x$ by the product of the scalars (and the property is that this is the same result, note that they are not the same object [$\color{lime}\textbf{Q}$. Not the same in which sense?]).

[6] Distributive $\bullet$ (in $\mathbb{K}$): $\forall \ \alpha,\beta \in \mathbb{K}, \forall \ x\in X$

$$(\alpha\stackrel{(1)}{+} \beta)\cdot x=\alpha x\stackrel{(2)}{+} \beta x$$

where (1) is a sum in $\mathbb{K}$ (so $\alpha+\beta$ is an element in $\mathbb{K}$) and (2) is a sum in $X$. (we compare this to the sum between $\alpha x$ and $\beta x$), and what happens is that those elements are the same. Note: I can do the opposite (see below).

[7] Distributive scalar: $\forall \ \alpha\in \mathbb{K}, \forall \ x,y\in X$ we have $\alpha\cdot (x+y)=\alpha\cdot x+\alpha\cdot y$. So we sum $x$ and $y$, this gives another element in $X$ that we can scale with $\alpha$ and this gives the same result as scaling both $x$ and $y$ by $\alpha$ and then summing $\alpha x$ and $\alpha y$. This is the idea.

[8] Neutral element for $\bullet$ (neutral element in $\mathbb{K}$): $1x=x, \forall \ x\in X$.

Given $\underline{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{\mathrm{T}}$, $\underline{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})^{\mathrm{T}}$. It is natural to define the sum in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ as: $$\begin{aligned} \underline{x}+\underline{y} & = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix} y_{1} \\ \vdots \\ y_{n} \end{bmatrix} & [\ \text{just notation}] & \ \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1}+y_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n}+y_{n} \end{bmatrix}\in \mathbb{R}^{n} \ \text{as sum of $n$ entries} \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} y_{1}+x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ y_{n}+x_{n} \end{bmatrix} & [\text{commutativity of $+$ in $\mathbb{R}$}] & \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} y_{1} \\ \vdots \\ y_{n} \end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix} & [\text{def. sum in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$}] & \\ & = \underline{y}+\underline{x}\end{aligned}$$ so we actually showed commutativity of $+$. Closed under scalar multiplication: $$\alpha \underline{x}=\alpha \begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix} \alpha x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ \alpha x_{n} \end{bmatrix}$$ which is a vector of $n$ entries ie. $\in \mathbb{R}^{n}$. Associativity of $+$: $$\begin{aligned} (\underline{x}+\underline{y})+\underline{z} & = \left(\begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix} +\begin{bmatrix} y_{1} \\ \vdots \\ y_{n} \end{bmatrix}\right)+\begin{bmatrix} z_{1} \\ \vdots \\ z_{n} \end{bmatrix} & [\text{notation}] & \ \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1}+y_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n}+y_{n} \end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix} z_{1} \\ \vdots \\ z_{n} \end{bmatrix}& [\text{def. $\underline{x}+\underline{y}$}] \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} (x_{1}+y_{1})+z_{1} \\ \vdots \\ (x_{n}+y_{n})+z_{n} \end{bmatrix} & [\text{def. + in } \mathbb{R}^{n}] & \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1}+(y_{1}+z_{1}) \\ \vdots \\ x_{n}+(y_{n}+z_{n}) \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}$$ by the associative property of the sum in $\mathbb{R}$, recall $(a+b)+c=a+(b+c)$, then $$\begin{aligned} & = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix} y_{1}+z_{1} \\ \vdots \\ y_{n}+z_{n} \end{bmatrix}& [\text{def. of + in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ btw. $\underline{x}$ and $(\underline{y}+\underline{z})$}] & \ \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix}+\left(\begin{bmatrix} y_{1} \\ \vdots \\ y_{n} \end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix} z_{1} \\ \vdots \\ z_{n} \end{bmatrix}\right) & [\text{but by def. this is...}] & \\ & = \underline{x}+(\underline{y}+\underline{z}) \end{aligned}$$ Additive identity. Let $\underline{0}=(0,\ldots,0)^{\mathrm{T}}\in \mathbb{R}^{n}$. Then $$\begin{aligned} \underline{x}+\underline{0} & = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}& [\text{notation of $\underline{x}$ and $\underline{0}$}] & \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1}+0 \\ \vdots \\ x_{n}+0 \end{bmatrix}& [\text{neutral el of + in }\mathbb{R}] & \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix} =\underline{x}\end{aligned}$$ Additive inverse. Def. $-\underline{x}=[-x_{1},\ldots, -x_{n}]^{\mathrm{T}}$. Then $$\begin{aligned} \underline{x}+(-\underline{x}) & = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix} -x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ -x_{n} \end{bmatrix}& [\text{notation/def. of }\underline{x}\text{ and }-\underline{x} ] & \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1}-x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n}-x_{n} \end{bmatrix}& [\text{inverse pr. of + in }\mathbb{R}, \ a+(-a)=0] & \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}=\underline{0}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}} \end{aligned}$$ Distributive +. We want to show $\alpha(\underline{x}+\underline{y})=\alpha\underline{x}+\alpha\underline{y}$. We have: $$\begin{aligned} \alpha(\underline{x}+\underline{y}) & = \alpha\left(\begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix} y_{1} \\ \vdots \\ y_{n} \end{bmatrix}\right) & [\text{notation}] & \\ & = \alpha \begin{bmatrix} x_{1}+y_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n}+y_{n} \end{bmatrix} & [\text{def. scalar mult. in }\mathbb{R}^{n}] & \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha(x_{1}+y_{1}) \\ \vdots \\ \alpha(x_{n}+y_{n}) \end{bmatrix} & [\text{distributive pr. of + in }\mathbb{R}^{n}] & \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha x_{1}+\alpha y_{1} \\ \vdots \\ \alpha x_{n}+\alpha y_{n} \end{bmatrix} & [\text{def. of + in }\mathbb{R}^{n}] & \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ \alpha x_{n} \end{bmatrix} +\begin{bmatrix} \alpha y_{1} \\ \vdots \\ \alpha y_{n} \end{bmatrix} =\alpha \underline{x}+\alpha \underline{y}& [\text{def. of scalar mult. in }\mathbb{R}^{n}] & \end{aligned}$$ Distributive $\bullet$. We want to show $\underline{x}(\alpha+\beta)=\alpha \underline{x}+\beta\underline{x}, \forall \ \alpha,\beta\in \mathbb{R}$. We have: $$\begin{aligned} \underline{x}(\alpha+\beta) & = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix} (\alpha+\beta)\end{aligned}$$ by definition of scalar multiplication in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ this is $$=\begin{bmatrix} x_{1}(\alpha+\beta) \\ \vdots \\ x_{n}(\alpha+\beta) \end{bmatrix}$$ then by the distributive property of $\bullet$ in $\mathbb{R}$ this means $$=\begin{bmatrix} \alpha x_{1}+\beta x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ \alpha x_{n}+\beta x_{n} \end{bmatrix}$$ and by definition of + in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ this can be seen as $$=\begin{bmatrix} \alpha x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ \alpha x_{n} \end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix} \beta x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ \beta x_{n} \end{bmatrix}=\alpha \underline{x}+\beta \underline{x}$$ Associativity of $\bullet$. We have to show $\alpha(\beta\underline{x})=(\alpha\beta)\underline{x}$. We have: $$\begin{aligned} \alpha(\beta\underline{x}) & = \alpha\left(\beta\begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix}\right)& [\text{notation}] & \\ & = \alpha\left(\begin{bmatrix} \beta x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ \beta x_{n} \end{bmatrix}\right)& [\text{scalar multiplication }\beta\underline{x}] & \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha(\beta x_{1}) \\ \vdots \\ \alpha(\beta x_{n}) \end{bmatrix}& [\text{def. scalar mult. $\alpha( \cdot)$}] & \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} (\alpha\beta)x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ (\alpha\beta)x_{n} \end{bmatrix}& [\text{def. scal. mult.}(\alpha\beta)\underline{x}] & \\ & = (\alpha\beta)\begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix} \\ & = (\alpha\beta)\underline{x} \end{aligned}$$ Finally, we check the identity for $\bullet$. Have to prove $1\underline{x}=\underline{x}$. We have $$\begin{aligned} 1\underline{x} & = 1\begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix}& [\text{def. scal. mult. 1}\underline{x}] & \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} 1x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ 1x_{n} \end{bmatrix}& [\text{identity in }\mathbb{R}] & \\ & = \underline{x} \end{aligned}$$ Therefore $(\mathbb{R}^{n}, +,\bullet)$ is a vector space. $\bigstar$
