A normed space is a space on which you can define a norm. Now, we should try to understand what is a space and what is a norm. Before diving in, note that this is not the most general possible framework. More general are topological spaces, inside them we find metric spaces. But since they present some strange pathological things we start directly from the even smaller subset of normed spaces.

We assume the space (set) of elements we are dealing with is a vector space. In practice we will always just use $\mathbb{R}$ vector spaces, but the theory can be extended for $\mathbb{C}$.

What is a vector space? A collection of elements with two operations.

[D.][Vector Space]
A $\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}$ (or $\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}$) vector space, where $\mathbb{K}$ is a field and $X$ is a non-empty set on which we can define two operations:
  • Sum (addition): given $x,y\in X$ we can define a third element $x+y\in X$ which is the sum of $x$ and $y$. Note: the third element “$x+y$” is treated as a single entity/object, if you want you can call it $z=x+y$.
  • Scalar multiplication: given $X\in X, \alpha\in \mathbb{K}$ we can define $\alpha\cdot x\in X$ as the product of $\alpha\in \mathbb{K}$ with $x\in X$. Again, the idea is that $\alpha \cdot x$ is a new single element and it still belongs to $X$. This view will be very useful in the exercises we will see below.

These operations satisfy the following properties (note that these are part of the definition):
[0] Closed $+$ and $\bullet$: if you take $x\in X,y\in X$ the operation is still inside the set ie. $x+y\in X$ and if $x\in X,\alpha\in \mathbb{K}\Rightarrow \alpha\cdot x\in X$ (ie. all elements are still inside the set when scaled).

[1] Commutativity of $+$: $x+y=y+x\ \forall \ x,y\in X$. So if you first sum one or the other at the end you have the same element. Note that many, operations in life are not commutative. It’s not the same if you put first your underwear and then your pants than the other way around. In the first case you will be considered as a normal person. In the second a bizarre or crazy one.

[2] Associativity of $+$: $(x+y)+z=x+(y+z)\ \forall \ z,y\in X$. Ie. the order of the sum doesn’t even count if you make the sum of 3 or more elements.

[3] Neutral element of $\bullet$: there is a neutral element with respect to the sum that we call $0_{X}\in X$ “the zero of $X$” or “the origin of the space $X$” such that $0_{X}+x=x+0_{X}=x,\ \forall \ x\in X$. So if you sum it you get the element itself.

[4] Unique opposite: there is a unique element $y=-x\in X$ (this is just notation) such that $x+y=0_{X}=y+x\ \forall \ x\in X$. Or in other words $x+z_{x}=0_{X}=z_{x}+x$ and we write $z_{x}=-x$. There is no subtraction, just the sum of the opposite. [$\color{lime}\textbf{Q}$. In which sense? What is the difference in practice?]

[5] Commutativity of the product $\bullet$:

$$\alpha \stackrel{(1)}{\cdot } (\beta \stackrel{(2)}{\cdot } x)=(\alpha \stackrel{(3)}{\cdot } \beta)\stackrel{(4)}{\cdot }x$$

where (1) is a product in $X$, (2) is a product in $X$, (3) is a product in $\mathbb{K}$ and (4) is a product in $X$ [$\color{lime}\textbf{Q}$. What does it mean?] On the left we scale $x$ by $\beta$ and then scale $\beta x$ with $\alpha$. On the right side we multiply the scalars and then scale $x$ by the product of the scalars (and the property is that this is the same result, note that they are not the same object [$\color{lime}\textbf{Q}$. Not the same in which sense?]).

[6] Distributive $\bullet$ (in $\mathbb{K}$): $\forall \ \alpha,\beta \in \mathbb{K}, \forall \ x\in X$

$$(\alpha\stackrel{(1)}{+} \beta)\cdot x=\alpha x\stackrel{(2)}{+} \beta x$$

where (1) is a sum in $\mathbb{K}$ (so $\alpha+\beta$ is an element in $\mathbb{K}$) and (2) is a sum in $X$. (we compare this to the sum between $\alpha x$ and $\beta x$), and what happens is that those elements are the same. Note: I can do the opposite (see below).

[7] Distributive scalar: $\forall \ \alpha\in \mathbb{K}, \forall \ x,y\in X$ we have $\alpha\cdot (x+y)=\alpha\cdot x+\alpha\cdot y$. So we sum $x$ and $y$, this gives another element in $X$ that we can scale with $\alpha$ and this gives the same result as scaling both $x$ and $y$ by $\alpha$ and then summing $\alpha x$ and $\alpha y$. This is the idea.

[8] Neutral element for $\bullet$ (neutral element in $\mathbb{K}$): $1x=x, \forall \ x\in X$.

[Eg.][$\mathbb{R}^{n}$ is a vsp.]
Given $\underline{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{\mathrm{T}}$, $\underline{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})^{\mathrm{T}}$. It is natural to define the sum in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ as: $$\begin{aligned} \underline{x}+\underline{y} & = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix} y_{1} \\ \vdots \\ y_{n} \end{bmatrix} & [\ \text{just notation}] & \ \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1}+y_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n}+y_{n} \end{bmatrix}\in \mathbb{R}^{n} \ \text{as sum of $n$ entries} \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} y_{1}+x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ y_{n}+x_{n} \end{bmatrix} & [\text{commutativity of $+$ in $\mathbb{R}$}] & \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} y_{1} \\ \vdots \\ y_{n} \end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix} & [\text{def. sum in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$}] & \\ & = \underline{y}+\underline{x}\end{aligned}$$ so we actually showed commutativity of $+$. Closed under scalar multiplication: $$\alpha \underline{x}=\alpha \begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix} \alpha x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ \alpha x_{n} \end{bmatrix}$$ which is a vector of $n$ entries ie. $\in \mathbb{R}^{n}$. Associativity of $+$: $$\begin{aligned} (\underline{x}+\underline{y})+\underline{z} & = \left(\begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix} +\begin{bmatrix} y_{1} \\ \vdots \\ y_{n} \end{bmatrix}\right)+\begin{bmatrix} z_{1} \\ \vdots \\ z_{n} \end{bmatrix} & [\text{notation}] & \ \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1}+y_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n}+y_{n} \end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix} z_{1} \\ \vdots \\ z_{n} \end{bmatrix}& [\text{def. $\underline{x}+\underline{y}$}] \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} (x_{1}+y_{1})+z_{1} \\ \vdots \\ (x_{n}+y_{n})+z_{n} \end{bmatrix} & [\text{def. + in } \mathbb{R}^{n}] & \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1}+(y_{1}+z_{1}) \\ \vdots \\ x_{n}+(y_{n}+z_{n}) \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}$$ by the associative property of the sum in $\mathbb{R}$, recall $(a+b)+c=a+(b+c)$, then $$\begin{aligned} & = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix} y_{1}+z_{1} \\ \vdots \\ y_{n}+z_{n} \end{bmatrix}& [\text{def. of + in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ btw. $\underline{x}$ and $(\underline{y}+\underline{z})$}] & \ \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix}+\left(\begin{bmatrix} y_{1} \\ \vdots \\ y_{n} \end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix} z_{1} \\ \vdots \\ z_{n} \end{bmatrix}\right) & [\text{but by def. this is...}] & \\ & = \underline{x}+(\underline{y}+\underline{z}) \end{aligned}$$ Additive identity. Let $\underline{0}=(0,\ldots,0)^{\mathrm{T}}\in \mathbb{R}^{n}$. Then $$\begin{aligned} \underline{x}+\underline{0} & = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}& [\text{notation of $\underline{x}$ and $\underline{0}$}] & \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1}+0 \\ \vdots \\ x_{n}+0 \end{bmatrix}& [\text{neutral el of + in }\mathbb{R}] & \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix} =\underline{x}\end{aligned}$$ Additive inverse. Def. $-\underline{x}=[-x_{1},\ldots, -x_{n}]^{\mathrm{T}}$. Then $$\begin{aligned} \underline{x}+(-\underline{x}) & = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix} -x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ -x_{n} \end{bmatrix}& [\text{notation/def. of }\underline{x}\text{ and }-\underline{x} ] & \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1}-x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n}-x_{n} \end{bmatrix}& [\text{inverse pr. of + in }\mathbb{R}, \ a+(-a)=0] & \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}=\underline{0}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}} \end{aligned}$$ Distributive +. We want to show $\alpha(\underline{x}+\underline{y})=\alpha\underline{x}+\alpha\underline{y}$. We have: $$\begin{aligned} \alpha(\underline{x}+\underline{y}) & = \alpha\left(\begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix} y_{1} \\ \vdots \\ y_{n} \end{bmatrix}\right) & [\text{notation}] & \\ & = \alpha \begin{bmatrix} x_{1}+y_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n}+y_{n} \end{bmatrix} & [\text{def. scalar mult. in }\mathbb{R}^{n}] & \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha(x_{1}+y_{1}) \\ \vdots \\ \alpha(x_{n}+y_{n}) \end{bmatrix} & [\text{distributive pr. of + in }\mathbb{R}^{n}] & \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha x_{1}+\alpha y_{1} \\ \vdots \\ \alpha x_{n}+\alpha y_{n} \end{bmatrix} & [\text{def. of + in }\mathbb{R}^{n}] & \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ \alpha x_{n} \end{bmatrix} +\begin{bmatrix} \alpha y_{1} \\ \vdots \\ \alpha y_{n} \end{bmatrix} =\alpha \underline{x}+\alpha \underline{y}& [\text{def. of scalar mult. in }\mathbb{R}^{n}] & \end{aligned}$$ Distributive $\bullet$. We want to show $\underline{x}(\alpha+\beta)=\alpha \underline{x}+\beta\underline{x}, \forall \ \alpha,\beta\in \mathbb{R}$. We have: $$\begin{aligned} \underline{x}(\alpha+\beta) & = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix} (\alpha+\beta)\end{aligned}$$ by definition of scalar multiplication in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ this is $$=\begin{bmatrix} x_{1}(\alpha+\beta) \\ \vdots \\ x_{n}(\alpha+\beta) \end{bmatrix}$$ then by the distributive property of $\bullet$ in $\mathbb{R}$ this means $$=\begin{bmatrix} \alpha x_{1}+\beta x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ \alpha x_{n}+\beta x_{n} \end{bmatrix}$$ and by definition of + in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ this can be seen as $$=\begin{bmatrix} \alpha x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ \alpha x_{n} \end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix} \beta x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ \beta x_{n} \end{bmatrix}=\alpha \underline{x}+\beta \underline{x}$$ Associativity of $\bullet$. We have to show $\alpha(\beta\underline{x})=(\alpha\beta)\underline{x}$. We have: $$\begin{aligned} \alpha(\beta\underline{x}) & = \alpha\left(\beta\begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix}\right)& [\text{notation}] & \\ & = \alpha\left(\begin{bmatrix} \beta x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ \beta x_{n} \end{bmatrix}\right)& [\text{scalar multiplication }\beta\underline{x}] & \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha(\beta x_{1}) \\ \vdots \\ \alpha(\beta x_{n}) \end{bmatrix}& [\text{def. scalar mult. $\alpha( \cdot)$}] & \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} (\alpha\beta)x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ (\alpha\beta)x_{n} \end{bmatrix}& [\text{def. scal. mult.}(\alpha\beta)\underline{x}] & \\ & = (\alpha\beta)\begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix} \\ & = (\alpha\beta)\underline{x} \end{aligned}$$ Finally, we check the identity for $\bullet$. Have to prove $1\underline{x}=\underline{x}$. We have $$\begin{aligned} 1\underline{x} & = 1\begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix}& [\text{def. scal. mult. 1}\underline{x}] & \\ & = \begin{bmatrix} 1x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ 1x_{n} \end{bmatrix}& [\text{identity in }\mathbb{R}] & \\ & = \underline{x} \end{aligned}$$ Therefore $(\mathbb{R}^{n}, +,\bullet)$ is a vector space. $\bigstar$

[D.][Seminorm and Norm]
Given an $\mathbb{R}$ vector space $X$, a seminorm on $X$ is a function $\mathcal{N}:X\to \mathbb{R}$ (note it takes values in $\mathbb{R}$ so it must be finite) such that: [1] Non-negative: $\mathcal{N}(x)\geq 0, \forall \ x\in X$ (and finite) [2] Homogeneous: $\mathcal{N}(\alpha \cdot x)=|\alpha|\mathcal{N}(x)$, $\forall \ x\in X,\alpha\in \mathbb{R}$. Note that $\alpha \mathcal{N}(x)$ would not be a seminorm if $\alpha$ was negative, this is why we need the absolute value. (Homogeneous of degree 1 if you want). [3] Triangle inequality: $\mathcal{N}(x+y)\leq \mathcal{N}(x)+\mathcal{N}(y)$, $\forall \ x,y\in X$. A seminorm is called a norm if it also satisfies: [4] Uniqueness of neutral element: $\mathcal{N}(x)=0 \Leftrightarrow x=0_{X}$ ie. the only element that has norm zero is the zero of the space.
Pair $(X,\lVert \rVert)$ normed space etc.

N. $\Leftarrow$ is always true, ie. if $x=0_{X}$ then its norm is always 0.

The idea of norm is basically to measures the magnitude of a vector, or the distance from the origin.

A measure is a function defined for sets. The norm is a function that assigns a number to a given element of a vector space. [$\color{lime}\textbf{Q}$. [Eg.][$(\mathbb{R},|\cdot |)$ is a normed space.]

Recall $$|a|=\begin{cases} a & a\geq 0 \\ -a & a<0 \end{cases}$$ Then: [1] Non-negative: $|x|\geq 0$ by definition. [2] Homogeneous: $|\alpha x|=|\alpha||x|$. This is a well known property of the absolute value that you may have seen in a course of real analysis. Note that if it was $\alpha=-1$ we would still have $|\alpha x|=|\alpha||x|$. [Eg.][$(\mathbb{R}^{n},\lVert \cdot \rVert_{e}$]
Let $X=\mathbb{R}^{n}=\{\underline{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}):x_{i}\in\mathbb{R},i=1,\ldots,n\}$. Consider the so called Euclidean norm $$\lVert\underline{x}\rVert=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}}=\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+x_{n}^{2}}$$ For example in $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ for $\underline{x}=(0,3,4)$ we have $$\lVert\underline{x}\rVert=\sqrt{0+3^{2}+4^{2}}=\sqrt{9+16}=\sqrt{25}=5$$ TODO: add figure. Then $(\mathbb{R}^{n},\lVert \cdot \rVert_{e})$ is a normed space. Indeed. [1] Nn: $\lVert\underline{x}\rVert\geq 0, \forall \ x\in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ ie. $\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}}\geq 0$ which is true by def. of $\sqrt{\cdot }$ for the square of real numbers (we can agree on this and take it for granted). [2] H: we have to show $\lVert \alpha \underline{x} \rVert=|\alpha|\lVert\underline{x}\rVert$. We know we have to start from here $$\lVert \alpha \underline{x} \rVert$$ what is this? Note that $\alpha \underline{x}$ is a single element for which you are computing the norm, ie. we are computing the norm for the element "$\alpha \underline{x}$" so what we have to do is plug it into the definition of the norm that we have set. Recall that in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ $$\alpha \underline{x}=\alpha\begin{bmatrix} x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ x_{n} \end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix} \alpha x_{1} \\ \vdots \\ \alpha x_{n} \end{bmatrix}$$ Thus $$\begin{aligned} \lVert \alpha \underline{x} \rVert & = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\alpha x_{i})^{2}} \\ & = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{2}x_{i}^{2}}& [\text{algebra: power of products}]\end{aligned}$$ Now, since $\alpha$ does not depend on $i$ we can take it out of the sum [$\color{lime}\textbf{Q}$. Why not $n\alpha^{2}$?] $$\begin{aligned} & = \sqrt{\alpha^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}} \\ & = |\alpha|\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}}& [\sqrt{\alpha^{2}}=|\alpha|] & \\ & = |\alpha|\lVert\underline{x}\rVert \end{aligned}$$ [4] Uq: $$\begin{aligned} \lVert\underline{x}\rVert=0 & \Leftrightarrow \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}}=0 \\ & \Leftrightarrow \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}=0 \\ & \Leftrightarrow x_{i}^{2}=0, \forall \ i=1,\ldots,n \\ & \Leftrightarrow x_{i}=0, \forall \ i=1,\ldots,n \end{aligned}$$ ie. the norm is 0 when all the components are 0, but if all the components are 0, this means $\underline{x}=(0,\ldots,0)$ which is the zero of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$. [3] Tq: we have to show $\lVert \underline{x}+\underline{y} \rVert\leq \lVert \underline{x} \rVert +\lVert \underline{y} \rVert$. Note that when we compute the norm of $\underline{x}+\underline{y}$ we treat $\underline{x}+\underline{y}$ as a single element, in other words we should write it as $$\lVert \underline{x}+\underline{y} \rVert=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}+y_{i})^{2}}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}}+\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{2}}+\sqrt{2 \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}y_{i}}$$ Many times (often also in exercises) it is easier to check this by taking the square. So the question becomes, $$\lVert \underline{x}+\underline{y} \rVert^{2}\leq (\lVert\underline{x}\rVert+\lVert\underline{y}\rVert)^{2}$$ is the above expresion true? If we show itis true then the triangle inequality follows by taking the square root on both sides. Let us then compute both quantities of interest, that is $$\lVert \underline{x}+\underline{y} \rVert^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}+y_{i})^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{2}+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}y_{i}=\lVert\underline{x}\rVert^{2} + \lVert\underline{y}\rVert^{2}+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}y_{i}$$ and $$(\lVert\underline{x}\rVert+\lVert\underline{y}\rVert)^{2}=\lVert\underline{x}\rVert^{2}+\lVert\underline{y}\rVert^{2}+2\lVert\underline{x}\rVert\lVert\underline{y}\rVert$$ If we show $2\lVert\underline{x}\rVert\lVert\underline{y}\rVert\geq 2\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}y_{i}$ for all $\underline{x},\underline{y}\in X$ then the triangle inequality would hold. For simplicity denote $\langle x,y \rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}y_{i}$. We show $|\langle x,y \rangle|\leq \lVert\underline{x}\rVert\lVert\underline{y}\rVert, \forall \ \underline{x},\underline{y}\in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ (this is Cauchy-Schwarz inequality). Set $g(t)=\lVert \underline{x}+t \underline{y} \rVert^{2}$ with $t \in\mathbb{R}$ and $\underline{x},\underline{y}$ fixed. Then: $$\begin{aligned} 0 \leq g(t) & = \lVert \underline{x}+t \underline{y} \rVert^{2} \\ & = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}+t y_{i})^{2} & [\text{expand the square}] & \\ & = \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}+t ^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{2}+2t \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}y_{i} \\ & = \lVert\underline{x}\rVert^{2}+t^{2}\lVert\underline{y}\rVert^{2}+2t \langle x,y \rangle \end{aligned}$$ Let $a=\lVert\underline{y}\rVert^{2}, b=\langle x,y \rangle, c=\lVert\underline{x}\rVert^{2}$ st. $0\leq g(t)=at^{2}+b t +c, \forall \ t\in \mathbb{R}$ ie. an upward quadratic function (parabola) st. $\Delta \leq 0$. This implies $\Delta =b^{2}-4ac\leq 0$ st. $4\langle x,y \rangle^{2}-4\lVert\underline{x}\rVert^{2}\lVert\underline{y}\rVert^{2}\leq 0$ so that $\langle x,y \rangle^{2}\leq \lVert\underline{x}\rVert^{2}\lVert\underline{y}\rVert^{2}$ (so this was the proof for CS inequality). Hence since $\langle x,y \rangle^{2}\leq \lVert\underline{x}\rVert^{2}\lVert\underline{y}\rVert^{2}$ we have $$\begin{aligned} \lVert \underline{x}+\underline{y} \rVert^{2} & = \lVert\underline{x}\rVert^{2}+\lVert\underline{y}\rVert^{2}+2 \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}y_{i}}_{\langle x,y \rangle} \\ & \stackrel{\text{CS}}{\leq } \lVert\underline{x}\rVert^{2}+\lVert\underline{y}\rVert^{2}+2\lVert\underline{x}\rVert\lVert\underline{y}\rVert\\ & = (\lVert\underline{x}\rVert+\lVert\underline{y}\rVert)^{2}\end{aligned}$$ and taking the $\sqrt{\cdot }$ we obtain the that the triangle inequality is verified. Thus $(\mathbb{R}^{n},\lVert \cdot \rVert_{e})$ is a normed space. $\bigstar$

This was our first challenging proof, but at the end it is again a matter of correctly knowing the definitions and using the trick with Cauchy-Schwarz.

Still on $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ we can define other norms. [$\color{lime}\textbf{Q}$. Example of non-norm for $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ ie. that doesn’t make it a normed space?]

[Eg.][($\mathbb{R}^{n}$, $\lVert \cdot \rVert_{1}$)]
Let $\lVert \underline{x} \rVert_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|$. So, the norm of a vector is the sum of the magnitude of the components. For example for $\underline{x}=(0,3,4)$ its norm would be $$\lVert \underline{x} \rVert=|0|+|3|+|4|=|7|=7$$ Does this $\lVert \underline{x} \rVert_{1}$ that we defined verify the properties of a norm? Let's check. [1] N: $\lVert \underline{x} \rVert_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|$ is a sum of absolute values, which are all non-negative so $\lVert \underline{x} \rVert\geq 0 \forall \ \underline{x}\in \mathbb{R}^{n}$. [2] H: $$\begin{aligned} \lVert \alpha \underline{x} \rVert_{1} & = \sum_{i=1}^{n}|\alpha x_{i}| & [\text{def. of } \lVert \cdot \rVert_{1}] & \\ & = \sum_{i=1}^{n}|\alpha||x_{i}| & [\text{pr. of }|\cdot |] & \\ & = |\alpha|\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}| & [\text{since }\alpha \text{ dnd. on $i$}] & \\ & = |\alpha|\lVert \underline{x} \rVert_{1} \end{aligned}$$ [3] T: we have $\lVert \underline{x}+\underline{y} \rVert_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}+y_{i}|$ and since $\forall \ i$ we have $|x_{i}+y_{i}|\leq |x_{i}|+|y_{i}|$ then $$\begin{aligned} \lVert \underline{x}+\underline{y} \rVert_{1} & = \sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}y_{i}| \\ & \leq \sum_{i=1}^{n}(|x_{i}|+|y_{i}|)\\ & = \sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|+\sum_{i=1}^{n}|y_{i}| \\ & = \lVert\underline{x}\rVert_{1}+\lVert\underline{y}\rVert_{1} \end{aligned}$$ [4] U: $\lVert \underline{x} \rVert_{1}=0$ means that $\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|=0$ but this happens whenever $x_{i}=0$ for all $i=1,\ldots,n$ ie. when $\underline{x}=(0,\ldots,0)=0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}$.
[Eg.][$(\mathbb{R}^{n},\lVert \cdot \rVert_{\infty})$ ]
Let $\lVert\underline{x}\rVert_{\infty}=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|x_{i}|$ that is the norm of a vector is the largest component. For our usual example $\underline{x}=(0,3,4)$ its infinity norm is 4. Let's check that this is also a norm on $\mathbb{R}^{n}$. [1] N: $\lVert\underline{x}\rVert_{\infty}=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|x_{i}|$ all the $|x_{i}|\geq 0$ thus the max is also $\geq 0$ as well. [2] H: $$\lVert \alpha \underline{x} \rVert_{\infty} =\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|\alpha x_{i}|$$ now, since $|\alpha x|=|\alpha||x|$ (this is a property of the absolute value) we can write this as $$\begin{aligned} & = \max_{1\leq i\leq n}|\alpha||x_{i}| \\ & = |\alpha|\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|x_{i}|& [\text{since $\alpha$ dnd on i}] & \\ & = |\alpha|\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\lVert \underline{x} \rVert_{\infty} \end{aligned}$$ [4] U: $$\begin{aligned} \lVert\underline{x}\rVert_{\infty} & = \max_{1\leq i\leq n}|x_{i}|=0 \\ & \Leftrightarrow |x_{i}|=0, \forall \ i=1,\ldots,n \\ & \Leftrightarrow x_{i}=0, \forall \ i=1,\ldots,n \end{aligned}$$ ie. the norm is zero only when $\underline{x}=(0,\ldots,0)=0_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}$. [3] T: $$\begin{aligned} \lVert \underline{x}+\underline{y} \rVert_{\infty} & = \max_{1\leq i\leq n}|x_{i}+y_{i}|& [\text{def. of }\lVert \cdot \rVert_{\infty}] & \\ & \leq \max_{1\leq i\leq n}(|x_{i}|+|y_{i}|) \end{aligned}$$ because $|x+y|\leq |x|+|y|$, and now, since $\forall \ i$ one has that $x_{i}+y_{i}\leq \max x_{i}+\max y_{i}$ this is $$\begin{aligned} & = \max_{1\leq i\leq n}|x_{i}|+\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|y_{i}| \\ & = \lVert \underline{x} \rVert_{\infty}+\lVert \underline{y} \rVert_{\infty} \end{aligned}$$

N. The infinity norm is also called supremum norm or uniform norm. E. Make ceg. for all these norms in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ and $\mathbb{R}^{3}$.

Now, what if instead of vectors with a finite number of components we take infinite vectors? Can we still form a normed space? The answer is yes, but we need some condition on the infinite vector that we will see now.

[Eg.][Space of sequences]
Consider $\underline{x}=(x_{n})_{n}$ and $\underline{y}=(y_{n})_{n}$ real sequences. Define the sum as $$\underline{x}+\underline{y}=(x_{n}+y_{n})_{n}$$ and the scalar product as $$\alpha \underline{x}=(\alpha x_{n})_{n}$$ Now call $$X:=\ell^{1}(\mathbb{N})=\{\underline{x}=(x_{n})\subset \mathbb{R}:\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|x_{i}|<\infty\}$$ ie. the set of sequences which are absolutely summable, or if you want the set of infinite sequences such that the sum of all its components is finite. Note that for example the sequence $(x_{n})=(\frac{1}{n})\notin \ell^{1}(\mathbb{N})$ because the sequence converges but the sum of the components if infinite. Before saying anything, it is important to observe that this is a vector space. Indeed we can check that it is closed for sum and scalar multiplication as follows. Consider $\underline{x}+\lambda \underline{y}=(x_{n}+ \lambda y_{n})$ for any $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$. Then $$\begin{aligned} \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|x_{n}+\lambda y_{n}| & \leq \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(|x_{n}|+|\lambda||y_{n}|)\end{aligned}$$ this is again the classical triangular inequality for the absolute value and this can be rewrote as $$=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|x_{n}|+|\lambda|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|y_{n}|$$ but each of this is finite by assumption, ie. $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|x_{n}|<\infty$ because $\underline{x}\in \ell^{1}(\mathbb{N})$ by def., $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$ so it is finite and $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|y_{n}|<\infty$. Therefore $\underline{x}+\lambda\underline{y}\in \ell^{1}(\mathbb{N})$, making $\ell^{1}(\mathbb{N})$ a vector space.

We conclude this lecture with a final example. Did you remember $\mathscr{C}(I)$? We said it was a normed space. Can we also define norms on this space? Again, it is possible.

[Eg.][$\mathscr{C}(I)$ with $\lVert \cdot \rVert_{1}$ and $\lVert \cdot \rVert_{\infty}$]
Consider $X=\mathscr{C}(I)=\{f:I\to \mathbb{R} \text{ cnt.}\}$ with $I=[a,b]\subset \mathbb{R}$. Define $$\lVert f \rVert_{\infty}=\sup_{t\in I}|f(t)|\quad \lVert f \rVert_{1}=\int _{I}|f(t)|dt=\int_{[a,b]} |f(t)|dt=\int_{a}^{b} |f(t)|dt$$ Then $(X,\lVert \cdot \rVert_{1})$ and $(X,\lVert \cdot \rVert_{\infty})$ are normed spaces. Hints: $\lVert f \rVert_{\infty}<\infty$ [Weierstrass theorem for $I=[a,b]$], $\lVert f \rVert_{1}<\infty$.[classical result in calculus: Riemann integral of a cnt. function on a finite interval is well defined and finite]. In class we have seen the proof for uniqueness. [4] U: $$\begin{aligned} \lVert f \rVert_{\infty}=0 & \Leftrightarrow \sup_{t\in I}|f(t)|=0 \\ & \Leftrightarrow |f(t)|=0, \forall \ t\in I \\ & \Leftrightarrow f(t)=0, \forall \ t\in I \end{aligned}$$ ie. the supremum norm of $f$ is zero only when $f=0_{\mathscr{C}(I)}$ ie. when the function is equal to 0 at any point in the interval. $$\begin{aligned} \lVert f \rVert_{1}=0 & \Leftrightarrow \int_{a}^{b} |f(t)|dt=0 \\ & \Leftrightarrow |f(t)|=0, \forall \ t\in [a,b] \\ & \Leftrightarrow f(t)=0, \forall \ t\in [a,b] \end{aligned}$$ ie. $f=0_{\mathscr{C}(I)}$.
