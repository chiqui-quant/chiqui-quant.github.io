Motivation

The following is a sequence of lecture notes from the course of Functional Analysis B(asic) from the M.Sc. in Stochastics and Data Science, given by prof. Bertrand Lods during 2021/2022. The motivation for publishing them in this blog serves multiple purposes:

  • (1) to review what I think was one of the most challenging yet enjoyable courses I ever took
  • (2) to learn and practice a bit more of git version control in general
  • (3) to find the right balance between “overly verbal” and “puzzling condensed” notes.

Regarding (1), I noticed that by reviewing the entire course material from start to finish multiple times usually allows me to understand better some parts or add and clarify details that I missed. For instance, now that I have some more experience and familiarity with the subject, almost two years, when I reviewed the very first lecture the other day, I was able to solve the proposed exercises, for which I felt completely lost the first time I wrote them down.

About (2), any time I review the material I try to think of ways to improve it. Either by adding examples, details or better explanations. Because of this, I am of the idea that one has “never finished” to learn a given subject and the purpose of using git in this case is to exploit this vision of perpetual revision, while keeping at the same time a good and solid grounding structure to start from.

Finally, to clarify point (3), you need to know I struggled a lot during the M.Sc. Because of my fragil math foundation from high-school and undergraduate years, it took me a very long time to learn to even just read math. Thus, the only option I found at the beginning (even if extremely suboptimal and crazy) was to literally write down every single word the professor said, along with the notation in order to learn how to properly read and interpret it. This has led to almost a hundred handwritten pages of words. Among all the problems this approach had, there is clearly the fact that to review the material was practically impossible.

On the other hand, it is easy to find textbooks and lecture notes with are basically a pile of math formulas, with little commentaries if you are lucky. Here is where this work comes into play. As you may have noticed, I borrowed the “Long Form” title from Jay Cummings, which has some wonderful books on proofs and real analysis. I believe his approach illustrates how math should be really taught and this is why I will try my best to replicate his approach for these notes.

Disclaimer: take into account the fact that 95 to 99% of the material and words you will encounter are taken almost verbatim from prof. Lods. He’s been taching this course for many years and one of the things I admired the most about him was that he tried his best to explain everything very clearly and in detail, making also funny commentaries from time to time and slowing down if necessary (in my case it was even the necessary was not sufficient many times).

Note: if you see parts like [$\color{lime}\textbf{Q}$. What is this note about?] It is because that is my way of annotating questions for what is not very clear to me yet.

What is functional analysis?

Functional analysis can be considered or summarized as the study of “function spaces” or “spaces of functions” and their properties. A space (in maths) is just another name for a set with some particular structure (generally a vector space structure, that is, closed under summation and scalar multiplication). This means we will consider sets of functions with some common property (eg. integrable or continuous over some interval) and we will call this a space of functions. Then by means of a norm we will be able to somehow measure distances between functions. [$\color{lime}\textbf{Q}$. Why? Optimization problems? Other reasons? Related to differential equations, how? More rel. to Banach spaces for the completeness property to find solutions. How about the linear algebra part? Systems of infinite equations?]

Another interpretation of functional analysis is as the combination of real analysis and linear algebra but infinite dimensional spaces.
